1.5 Health financing and economic evaluation

  • Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) is household private spending, separate from government health expenditure. Its share of total health expenditure has fallen over successive National Health Accounts rounds, but medicines and diagnostics remain major household costs.
  • Question-bank context: historical estimates describe OOPE around 55–60%, government health spending around 1.2–1.5% of GDP and 6–7 crore people pushed below the poverty line annually. This is low compared with many developed systems spending 6–10%+ of GDP publicly; use the latest NHA for current Indian values.
  • Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) compares alternatives by cost per common natural outcome (e.g., cost per case prevented or life-year gained) to maximize outcome per rupee—not to select the most expensive intervention.
CEA ratio = Cost ÷ Natural health outcome  |  Prefer efficient incremental benefit, not lowest cost alone

4.3 Leprosy

4.4 Filariasis, malaria and rabies

ProgrammeHigh-yield facts
LF mass drug administrationAnnual community-wide round in endemic districts, including asymptomatic carriers; excludes children <2 years, pregnant women and severely ill persons. Selective night-blood testing would miss many carriers, so MDA is population based. Directly observed swallowing improves effective coverage. Monitoring includes night-blood-survey microfilaria rate, target <1%.
MalariaAnnual Parasite Incidence (API) monitors endemicity. P. vivax relapse prevention requires primaquine against hepatic hypnozoites, generally as a 14-day radical course after assessing G6PD-related safety; chloroquine clears blood stages but not hypnozoites. Artesunate is used in falciparum ACT; pyrimethamine is an antifolate partner, not radical cure.

Worked example (Q59): API = 2,500 ÷ 5,00,000 × 1,000 = 5.0 per 1,000. Under the categories supplied in the question (<1 low, 1–5 moderate, >5 high), exactly 5.0 lies at the upper boundary of moderate, not mathematically “>5.” Programme stratification cut-offs can differ, so follow the framework stated in the exam.
Eradication recall: Smallpox remains the only human disease eradicated worldwide. Do not join this fact to an unrelated measles herd-immunity reason.

6. Epidemiologic Measures

6.1 Incidence, prevalence and outbreak measures

MeasureDefinition / denominator
IncidenceNew cases arising in a defined period ÷ population at risk; measures risk. Do not add pre-existing cases.
PrevalenceAll existing cases at a point/period ÷ population; reflects disease burden.
Attack rateNew cases among an exposed/at-risk group during a time-limited outbreak ÷ total exposed/at risk × 100; operationally a cumulative incidence proportion.
Case fatality proportionDeaths due to disease ÷ diagnosed cases of that disease × 100; measures severity, not population mortality.
APIConfirmed malaria cases in a year per 1,000 population at risk.
Attack rate = Ill ÷ Exposed × 100  |  CFR = Deaths among cases ÷ Cases × 100
Worked example (Q19): Wedding attack rate = 50 ÷ 400 × 100 = 12.5%. The source also implies 4 deaths: CFR = 4 ÷ 50 × 100 = 8.0%.
Worked example (Q23): CFR = 3 deaths ÷ 75 cases × 100 = 4.0%. Attack rate = 75 ÷ 500 × 100 = 15.0%; deaths ÷ exposed = 0.6% is neither of these.
Worked example (Q67): Hostel attack rate = 50 ÷ 250 × 100 = 20.0%. The source’s alternate deaths/cases calculation gives CFR = 8.0%.
Worked example (Q82): Chicken-specific attack rate = 80 ÷ 200 × 100 = 40%; non-eaters = 10 ÷ 100 = 10%. The 30-point difference implicates chicken curry.

7. Screening & Study Designs

7.1 Test validity and predictive value

MeasureFormulaInterpretation
SensitivityTP ÷ (TP + FN)Proportion of truly diseased correctly positive; high sensitivity means few false negatives. SnNOut: a negative result on a very sensitive test helps rule out.
SpecificityTN ÷ (TN + FP)Proportion of truly non-diseased correctly negative; high specificity means few false positives and matters when false-positive consequences are serious. SpPIn: a positive result on a very specific test helps rule in.
PPVTP ÷ (TP + FP)Probability disease is present given a positive result; increases as prevalence rises.
NPVTN ÷ (TN + FN)Probability disease is absent given a negative result; generally rises as prevalence falls.
Worked example (Q122): In 100,000 people at 0.1% prevalence: diseased = 100 and non-diseased = 99,900. At 99% sensitivity, TP = 99; at 99% specificity, FP = 999. PPV = 99 ÷ 1,098 ≈ 9%, so about 91% of positive results are false positives. Low prevalence can overwhelm excellent sensitivity/specificity.
Validity looks inward; prediction looks outward: sensitivity/specificity condition on true disease status, while PPV/NPV condition on the observed test result and depend strongly on prevalence.

7.2 Wilson–Jungner screening criteria

  • Important health problem
  • Recognizable latent/early stage
  • Suitable, acceptable test
  • Accepted treatment available
  • Facilities for diagnosis/treatment
  • Agreed treatment policy
  • Natural history understood
  • Cost balanced
  • Continuing process
Ethical trap: Screening without an accepted beneficial treatment pathway can create anxiety and harm; test availability alone is insufficient.

7.3 Study designs

DesignDirection / useMain measure
Cross-sectionalExposure and outcome measured at the same time; estimates prevalence.Prevalence / prevalence ratio
Case-controlStart with cases and controls, then look back for exposure; design of choice for rare disease because it needs fewer participants.Odds ratio
CohortStart with exposed and unexposed, then compare incidence through follow-up; useful for rare exposures.RR, AR, incidence
Randomized controlled trialRandomly allocates intervention/control and follows outcomes; randomization balances known and unknown confounders on average and limits selection bias.RR, ARR, NNT
Assertion–reason caution: Two true statements do not automatically form an explanation. A reason must state the causal/logical basis of the assertion; randomization’s key explanatory role is allocation that reduces systematic baseline differences.

7.4 Association and treatment-effect measures

OR = (a × d) ÷ (b × c)  |  RR = IE ÷ IU  |  AR = IE − IU  |  NNT = 1 ÷ ARR
  • 2×2 case-control layout: a = exposed cases; b = exposed controls; c = unexposed cases; d = unexposed controls. OR >1 suggests increased odds, OR =1 no association and OR <1 protection. OR approximates RR when disease is rare (often taught as <10% incidence).
  • RR interpretation: RR >1 harmful/positive association; RR =1 no association; RR <1 protective. RR requires measurable incidence, as in cohort studies and RCTs—not ordinary case-control sampling.
  • Attributable risk: excess absolute risk among exposed that could be prevented if the causal exposure were removed.
  • NNT: NNT = 1/(CER − EER) for a beneficial event reduction; it is the patients to treat to prevent one additional adverse event. NNT=1 is ideal; very large NNT indicates small absolute benefit, but no fixed cut-off replaces clinical context and confidence intervals.
Worked example (Q91): Smokers: 20/100 = 20%; non-smokers: 4/200 = 2%. RR = 0.20 ÷ 0.02 = 10.0: smokers had ten times the risk in this cohort.
Worked interpretation (Q117): OR 1.5 with 95% CI 0.8–3.2 is not statistically significant at α=0.05 because the interval includes the null value 1.0.

8.3 Transmission dynamics

R₀ = Average secondary cases from one case in a fully susceptible population  |  Herd-immunity threshold = 1 − (1 ÷ R₀)
Worked interpretation (Q87): Measles R₀ ≈ 12–18 means one infectious person generates, on average, 12–18 secondary infections in a fully susceptible population—not that every patient always infects exactly that number.
Worked example (Q89): If measles R₀ = 15, threshold = 1 − 1/15 = 0.933 = 93.3% immune through vaccination or prior infection. Real programme coverage targets exceed 95% because vaccine effectiveness and delivery are imperfect; small coverage drops can permit outbreaks.
  • Endemic: constant/usual background presence in a defined area.
  • Epidemic: cases above the expected level in a community/region.
  • Dengue: mainly transmitted by day-biting Aedes aegypti, not night-biting Culex.

Jungalwalla (1967)Integration of preventive, promotive and curative services at all levels; unified cadre/common seniority and no private practice among related proposals.
Kartar Singh (1973)Multipurpose Worker scheme; convert single-purpose malaria, smallpox and family-planning workers. Classic committee recommendation: one male and one female health worker at each subcentre serving roughly 3,000–3,500, with a PHC around 50,000 and stronger supervision.
Srivastava (1975)Community health workers, ROME (Reorientation of Medical Education), referral service and a Medical/Health Education Commission for health-manpower and education reform.
Hathi (1975)Drug/pharmaceutical industry, essential drugs and stronger public-sector role—not the Srivastava recommendation.
Bajaj (1986)Health-manpower planning and development; proposed an Educational Commission for Health Sciences on UGC lines.
B–M–J–K–S–H–B: Bhore builds; Mudaliar strengthens; Jungalwalla integrates; Kartar makes MPWs; Srivastava creates community workers/ROME; Hathi handles drugs; Bajaj plans education/manpower.
