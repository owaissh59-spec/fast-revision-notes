2. Fixed & Volatile Oils

2.1 Fixed vs Volatile Oils – Comparison

FeatureFixed OilsVolatile Oils
ChemistryGlycerides of fatty acidsTerpenes/aromatics; NOT glycerides
Stain on paperPermanent greasy stainNo permanent stain (evaporates)
VolatilityNon-volatile at RTVolatile / evaporate at RT
SaponificationYes (by alkali)No
ExtractionCold/hot expression, solvent extraction (multiple methods)Steam distillation (preferred)
DetectionSaponification/acid/iodine valuesClevenger apparatus (hydrodistillation)
Q7: Fixed oils are NOT obtained exclusively by cold expression — they use multiple methods (cold/hot expression, solvent extraction). This is what distinguishes it as a wrong property.
Q12: Clevenger apparatus specifically detects/measures volatile oil content by hydrodistillation. Saponification/acid/iodine values are for fixed oils.

2.2 Analytical Values for Fixed Oils

ValueDefinitionIndicates
Iodine valueg of I₂ absorbed per 100 g oilDegree of unsaturation (more C=C → higher value). Drying oils >100; non-drying <100
Saponification valuemg KOH to saponify 1 g of fatAverage molecular weight of FAs. High value = shorter chain FAs (e.g., coconut oil/lauric acid)
Acid valuemg KOH to neutralize free FAs in 1 g oilDegree of rancidity/hydrolysis. High = more free fatty acids
Iodine = Unsaturation (I absorbs into double bonds); Acid value = Age/rancidity (free FAs accumulate over time).

2.3 Steam Distillation of Volatile Oils

Q21, Q28: Volatile oils require steam distillation because they decompose at their normal boiling points. Principle: two immiscible liquids boil below the BP of either component alone → extracts oil without thermal decomposition. Standard method for essential oil extraction.

  • Basic due to nitrogen lone pair; most are optically active & levorotatory.
  • Form soluble salts with mineral acids (HCl, H₂SO₄) — NOT insoluble.

Q26: "Alkaloids form insoluble salts with mineral acids" is INCORRECT. They form SOLUBLE salts with mineral acids; insoluble precipitates with alkaloidal reagents.

4. Pharmaceutics – Extraction, Distillation & Drying

4.1 Percolation vs Maceration

FeaturePercolationMaceration
PrincipleFresh solvent continuously contacts drug → maintains concentration gradientDrug soaked in solvent → equilibrium reached
EfficiencyMore complete extractionLess complete
Solvent useUses MORE solventUses less
Q16: Key advantage of percolation = fresh solvent maintains concentration gradient → more efficient than maceration (where equilibrium is eventually reached).
Q30 (Worked example): Percolation for 100 mL extract; marc retains 15 mL → Total menstruum needed = 100 + 15 = 115 mL.

4.2 Distillation Methods

MethodPrinciple / Use
Simple distillationRequires BP difference ≥25°C between components
Fractional distillationSeparates miscible liquids by BP differences; fractionating column provides multiple vaporization-condensation cycles (theoretical plates)
Steam distillationImmiscible liquids boil below either component's BP → volatile oils extracted without decomposition
Vacuum distillationReduced pressure lowers BP → prevents thermal decomposition of sensitive compounds
Q17, Q20: All three statements correct — simple needs ≥25°C BP diff; fractionating column = multiple theoretical plates; vacuum lowers BP preventing decomposition.

4.3 Evaporation

Q23 (Worked example): 500 mL of 10% w/v → 25% w/v. Solute = 10% × 500 = 50 g constant. For 25%: 50 g in X mL → X = 50/0.25 = 200 mL final volume (remove 300 mL solvent).

4.4 Spray Drying

"Spray drying is unsuitable for heat-sensitive materials" (Q25)INCORRECT — it CAN be used for heat-sensitive materialsRapid evaporation → evaporative cooling keeps particle temperature low; contact time = seconds. Used for vaccines & proteins.

5. Pharmaceutics – Weighing, Size Reduction & Emulsification

5.1 Prescription Balance

  • Class B (dispensing): Sensitivity requirement = 6 mg; Capacity = 120 g.
  • Analytical balance (Class A) = 0.1 mg sensitivity; Microbalance = ultra-precise research.
Minimum Weighable Qty (MWQ) = Sensitivity Requirement / Acceptable Error × 100
% Error = (Sensitivity Requirement / Quantity Weighed) × 100
Q5: SR = 6 mg, acceptable error = 5% → MWQ = 6/0.05 = 120 mg. Below 120 mg → aliquot method required.
Q11: Weighing 80 mg with SR 6 mg → % error = (6/80) × 100 = 7.5%. Exceeds 5% → use aliquot method.

5.2 IP Sieve Classification

Sieve number ↑ = aperture ↓. "85 = 180 μm = moderately fine" is a high-yield fact.

Q38: Both statements correct — pulverization by intervention uses volatile solvent; camphor is ground with alcohol (intervening agent) as it's waxy and hard to grind directly.

5.4 Emulsification – Dry Gum Method

Oil TypeOil : Water : Gum Ratio
Fixed oil (cod liver oil, olive)4 : 2 : 1
Volatile oil2 : 2 : 1
Mineral oil3 : 2 : 1
Q9 (Worked example): Cod liver oil 40 mL (fixed oil → 4:2:1). Water = (2/4) × 40 = 20 mL; Gum = (1/4) × 40 = 10 g. Method: triturate gum with oil, add ALL water at once with vigorous trituration.
Q32: Mineral oil ratio = 3:2:1 (harder to emulsify than fixed oils). For 200 mL mineral oil primary emulsion, gum ≈ 50 g by weight proportion.
Ratio memory: Fixed = 4:2:1 (easiest); Mineral = 3:2:1 (middle); Volatile = 2:2:1 (hardest). As difficulty ↑, oil portion ↓.

7.1 Classification of Incompatibilities

TypeOccursMechanismExamples
PhysicalIn vitro (in preparation)Immiscibility, liquefaction (eutectic), adsorption, insolubilityOil + water (no emulsifier); Camphor + Menthol (eutectic); Kaolin adsorbing alkaloids
ChemicalIn vitroOxidation, reduction, precipitation, effervescence, hydrolysisAdrenaline oxidation in alkali; AgNO₃ + NaCl; NaHCO₃ + acid

Q66: Physical incompatibility is NOT always visually detectable (e.g., adsorption is invisible). Chemical incompatibility involves redox reactions. Therapeutic incompatibility occurs in vivo. Not all incompatibilities resolved by vehicle change alone.

7.2 Physical Incompatibility – Key Types

Immiscibility (Q59)Two immiscible liquids prescribed without emulsifierOvercome by adding suitable emulsifying agent.

Adsorption (Q54)Kaolin / activated charcoal adsorbs alkaloidsLarge surface area reduces drug availability. Not visually detectable.

7.4 Therapeutic (Physiological) Incompatibility

Antagonism (Q52, Q55)CNS depressant + CNS stimulant; Morphine + NaloxoneOpposing pharmacological actions in vivo.
Synergism (Q82)Phenobarbitone + Chlorpromazine → excessive CNS depressionBoth are CNS depressants → additive/synergistic sedation & respiratory depression.
Warfarin interactions (Q57)Aspirin enhances (↑ bleeding); Vitamin K antagonizes warfarinBoth are therapeutic incompatibilities occurring in vivo.

Q94: Emulsification overcomes immiscibility (✓); changing order of mixing can prevent precipitation (✓); absorbents overcome liquefaction (✓). But adsorbents do NOT overcome eutectic formation — that statement is WRONG.

8. Pharmacology – Dose, Potency & ADRs

8.1 Key Pharmacological Definitions

TermDefinition
PotencyAmount of drug needed to produce a given effect (lower dose = more potent); measured by EC₅₀/ED₅₀
EfficacyMaximum effect a drug can produce (regardless of dose)
Drug idiosyncrasyAbnormal genetically determined drug reaction; qualitatively different from expected; unrelated to dose
Drug cumulationRate of administration exceeds rate of elimination → progressive ↑ in plasma levels
TachyphylaxisRapid development of tolerance after repeated doses
Cross-toleranceTolerance to one drug extends to related drugs
Q63: Tolerance = decreased response requiring higher doses but does NOT inherently increase toxicity. Tachyphylaxis (✓), cross-tolerance (✓), and tolerance is decreased response (✓) are all correct.
Q67 (Case): Hepatotoxic drug → liver damage → impaired own metabolism → drug cumulation → worsening toxicity (positive feedback loop).
Q69: Cumulation = administration rate > elimination rate → progressive plasma level increase → potential toxicity.

8.2 Antidotes & Emergency Doses

AntidoteForDose
Atropine sulphateOP poisoning1.8–3 mg IV, repeat q5–10 min until atropinization
NaloxoneOpioid overdose0.4–2 mg IV, repeated
N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)Paracetamol overdose150 mg/kg IV initial loading
Pralidoxime (2-PAM)OP poisoning (reactivates AChE)1–2 g IV over 15–30 min

Universal antidote (Q65): Activated charcoal (adsorbent) + Magnesium oxide (neutralizes acids) + Tannic acid (precipitates alkaloids/metals). Largely replaced by charcoal alone.

8.3 Dose Modification in Renal Impairment

  • Drugs excreted mainly by kidneys need dose reduction (✓).
  • Creatinine clearance (CrCl) guides dosing decisions (✓).
  • Only drugs significantly eliminated renally need adjustment — hepatically metabolized drugs may NOT need renal dose adjustment.
  • "All drugs need dose adjustment in renal failure" is INCORRECT (Q79).

9.5 Displacement Value (DV)

DV = grams of drug that occupy the same volume as (displace) 1 g of base
Base to be removed = Weight of drug ÷ DV
S2·Q1: DV = 1.5 means 1.5 g of drug displaces 1 g of cocoa butter (same volume). Needed to calculate how much base to subtract.
S2·Q59: DV is most critical when the drug dose is large relative to the base. Small doses displace negligible base and can be ignored.

11.3 Ointment vs Paste vs Cream vs Liniment

FormDefining feature
OintmentSemi-solid preparation for topical application
Paste> 25% finely powdered solid → stiffer, less greasy, more absorptive & MORE protective; e.g. zinc oxide paste
CreamLighter emulsion-type semi-solid (o/w or w/o)
LinimentLiquid/semi-liquid applied by rubbing / friction
S2·Q33, S2·Q50, S2·Q62: Pastes = >25% solids, stiffer than an ointment of the same base, zinc oxide paste is the classic example. Trap: pastes are more (not less) protective against chemical irritants because the high solid load forms a physical barrier.

11.4 Methods of Ointment Preparation

FeatureFusion methodTrituration (incorporation)
When usedBase has waxy / different-melting-point components; drug is heat-stableHeat-sensitive drug; small-scale extemporaneous work
ProcedureMelt in decreasing order of melting point → stir continuously until coolFine powder → levigate with small quantity of base → geometric incorporation into cold base on slab/mortar
Volatile substancesAdded after mass has cooled sufficiently (prevents loss)Added directly (no heat)
S2·Q26, S2·Q61 (A & R both correct): Fusion is chosen when ingredients have different melting points; melting in decreasing order of melting point minimises heat exposure, then stir till cool for a uniform product.
S2·Q43 – the trap: volatile substances are added when the mass has cooled, NOT while hot.
S2·Q31, S2·Q44 – Levigation: reducing particle size by triturating the drug with a small quantity of levigating agent (the base itself or mineral oil) on an ointment slab → smooth, grit-free dispersion.

13. Mixtures & Dispensing Practice

13.1 Diffusible vs Indiffusible Solids

FeatureDiffusibleIndiffusible
Behaviour after shakingRemain suspended long enough to pour an accurate doseSettle rapidly (within ~2 min) → inaccurate dosing
Suspending agentNot requiredRequired (tragacanth mucilage, bentonite, compound tragacanth powder)
ExamplesLight magnesium carbonate, kaolin, light MgOPrecipitated chalk (CaCO₃), calamine, zinc oxide, heavy powders
S2·Q20, S2·Q40: Diffusible = briefly suspended, dose pourable. Precipitated chalk is indiffusible → needs tragacanth mucilage; light magnesium carbonate and kaolin are diffusible.

13.2 Order of Mixing

Dissolve soluble → triturate/levigate insoluble with suspending agent → combine → add volatile/flavouring oils LAST → make up to volume
S2·Q38, S2·Q41: Dissolve the soluble drug in part of the vehicle first; triturate the insoluble drug separately with suspending agent + remaining vehicle; then combine both portions with gentle mixing.
S2·Q63 (worked order): Dissolved sugar + suspended chalk + flavouring oil → dissolve sugar, suspend chalk with suspending agent, add oil last so the oil does not interfere with suspension formation.

13.3 Adjuvants – Flavour & Colour

PurposeAgents
Flavouring (paediatric, bitter drugs)Raspberry or orange syrup — fruit flavours preferred by children (S2·Q32)
Colouring – external useScarlet red, brilliant green dye (S2·Q36)
Colouring – internal useApproved food colours: amaranth (red), green S

13.4 Labelling of Dispensed Mixtures (S2·Q42)

  • Required: name & address of the pharmacy • clear directions for use • date of dispensing.
  • Trap: "Keep out of reach of children" is mandatory for most medicines — not optional.
  • Suspensions additionally require "Shake well before use".

13.5 Data Interpretation

S2·Q28: 200 mL mixture, Drug A 5% w/v (soluble) + Drug B 2% w/v (indiffusible, settles in 2 min) → add a suspending agent such as tragacanth mucilage to raise viscosity, retard sedimentation and keep dosing uniform.
S2·Q75: Calamine 5% + zinc oxide 3% (both indiffusible) = a calamine-lotion-type suspension → needs a suspending agent and a "Shake well before use" label.

14.3 Steam Inhalation Technique

S2·Q76 (both statements correct, but R does NOT explain A): Hot — not boiling — water is used chiefly to avoid scalding the patient and give a comfortable inhalation temperature, rather than to prevent decomposition.
